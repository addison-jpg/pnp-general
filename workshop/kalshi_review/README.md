# Prediction markets and public opinion: a literature review

Status: search-only review, four parts (A to D). No full texts were opened, and every finding is stated as the search summary reports it. No claim here is checked.

## Bottom line

- **No peer-reviewed study was found** that measures prediction markets (Kalshi, PredictIt, Polymarket) changing public opinion, belief or voter behaviour. (A)
- **Accuracy studies do exist** and are peer-reviewed or preprints: markets such as the Iowa Electronic Markets have beaten polls on some past US elections. This is about forecasting, not influence on opinion. (A)
- **The one opinion-influence study found** is a Brennan Center survey experiment: 1,994 registered voters, odds shown alongside candidate pairs; the summary reports that odds raised perceived electability. It is a report, not peer-reviewed, by an advocacy organisation, and its funder and vendor were not found. (A, B)
- **Public-opinion polls disagree.** Reuters/Ipsos: 60% oppose using markets to wager on elections. An Axis poll **commissioned by Kalshi**: 89% support. (B)
- **Industry calibration claims are self-published.** Kalshi reports its prices are "very well calibrated" across 2.2 million resolved markets, but the documents were not opened. The Coalition for Prediction Markets, whose members include Kalshi, claims markets beat polls by about 30%. (C)

## Part A: academic studies (sources listed in `A.md`)

- Berg, Nelson and Rietz (2008), *International Journal of Forecasting*: IEM markets beat national polls on US elections 1988 to 2004, more than 100 days out.
- Gruca and Rietz (2025), *PS: Political Science & Politics*: IEM forecasts for 2024. Abstract not read.
- Cutting et al. (2025), arXiv preprint: Polymarket outperformed polls in the 2024 election, especially in swing states.
- Tsang and Yang (2026), arXiv preprint: on-chain analysis of trading in the 2024 Polymarket election market. No voter data.
- Agent-based manipulation study (2026), arXiv preprint: simulated whales can move prices briefly. Simulation, not empirical.

## Part B: the polls (`B.md`)

| poll | n | funder | finding in the summary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brennan Center | 1,994 registered voters | not found beyond the publisher | odds raised perceived electability |
| Reuters/Ipsos | 4,506 adults | not found | 60% oppose, 13% support election wagering |
| Axis Research | 1,219 voters | **Kalshi** | 89% support prediction markets |
| Politico / Public First | 2,035 adults | none found | 30% say election-outcome betting should be legal |
| Scripps / Talker | 2,000 Americans | Scripps (commissioned) | doubt markets can stop insider trading |

Conflict: the Axis poll is the company's own, and its 89% figure is attached to different items in different sources. Exact question wording is missing for most polls.

## Part C: industry and trade-group publications (`C.md`)

- Kalshi calibration publication (2026): "very well calibrated" across 2,243,741 resolved markets. Not opened.
- Kalshi midterm working paper (2026): a preliminary calibration paper. Not read.
- Kalshi inflation-forecast claim: beats the Wall Street consensus. Rests on an internal analysis; no data seen.
- Coalition for Prediction Markets (December 2025 launch): claims markets beat polls by about 30%. Its aims include federal rules, which means it is making a policy argument.
- American Gaming Association (trade group opposed to prediction markets): a poll, by Qualtrics, reported as "8 in 10 say regulate as gambling." Topline not seen.

No independent review of any of these was found.

## Part D: concept readings (`D.md`), labelled analogy, no check

- **Panopticon** (Bentham 1791; Foucault, *Discipline and Punish*, 1975): visibility without a visible watcher makes subjects regulate themselves. Chapter confirmed; page numbers not.
- **Signs** (Saussure, Peirce): a sign links a form to a meaning by convention; Peirce's triadic version is contested as a mapping onto Saussure.
- **Simulacra** (Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation*, 1981): a model can come to replace the reality it was meant to measure.

Readings for prediction markets, each labelled as a reading:
- Reading: a published price works like the central tower of the panopticon, since traders may adjust to being watched without knowing who is reading.
- Reading: Foucault's self-discipline could map onto traders following a displayed price rather than their own information.
- Reading: Baudrillard's worry is that a price comes to stand in for the outcome it was meant to measure.

These are analogies. Nothing here tests them.

## Not claimed

- That prediction markets change public opinion. The one study that says so is a non-peer-reviewed report.
- That the Brennan survey generalises. It was a single snapshot in 2026.
- That any company claim about calibration is true. The documents were not read.
- Anything about cryptocurrency mining.
